How business barter works in India, and how GST treats it

Swapping a machine for stock, or warehouse space for finished goods, keeps cash in the business. Under GST, though, a swap is not tax-free: each side is making a sale. Here is how it works in practice.

DASKIES guide · 16 November 2026

What counts as barter

Barter is paying for something with goods or services instead of money. In Indian industry it often looks like a used machine given for a quantity of raw material, factory space given for finished products, or job work paid for with stock. Many swaps are partly cash: if one side's goods are worth more, the other side pays the difference.

On DASKIES, swaps can involve two, three or four businesses. In a three-party swap, A gives to B, B gives to C, and C gives back to A, with any gaps settled in cash.

Under GST, each side of a swap is a supply

Section 7(1)(a) of the CGST Act, 2017 defines "supply" to include barter and exchange made for consideration in the course of business. "Consideration" covers payment in money or otherwise. So when two registered businesses swap, there are two taxable supplies: each business supplies its goods or services to the other, and each one is liable for GST on what it gives.

In practice this means two tax invoices, one from each side, and two GST payments to the government. Even in a pure swap with no cash changing hands, plan the cash for the GST each side owes.

What value goes on the invoice

Normally GST is charged on the price paid. When the price is not wholly in money, Rule 27 of the CGST Rules sets the order for finding the value:

  1. the open market value of the supply;
  2. if that is not available, the cash paid plus the money value of the non-cash part, if that value is known at the time of supply;
  3. if that is not available, the value of goods or services of like kind and quality;
  4. failing those, the cost-based or residual methods in Rules 30 and 31.

The simplest way to stay on safe ground is to agree, in writing, a market value for each item in the swap before anything moves, backed by recent prices, quotes or a valuation report.

A worked example

Firm A gives Firm B a used CNC machine. Both agree its market value is ₹45 lakh. Firm B gives Firm A groundnut oil with a market value of ₹36 lakh, and pays ₹9 lakh in cash to settle the difference.

  • Firm A issues a tax invoice for the machine at ₹45 lakh, plus GST at the rate that applies to that machine.
  • Firm B issues a tax invoice for the oil at ₹36 lakh, plus GST at the rate that applies to that oil.
  • The ₹9 lakh cash payment settles the difference in value.
  • Each firm can claim input tax credit for the GST on what it received, if it is otherwise eligible.

If Firm A claimed input tax credit when it bought the machine, a special rule applies when it supplies the machine onward. See How to sell a used industrial machine in India.

Three- and four-party swaps

A chain of swaps is a set of separate supplies. Each business invoices the business it gives to, at the agreed value. Agree all the values and the cash adjustments in one written agreement signed by every party, so the invoices match and nobody is left carrying a gap.

Income tax

For income tax, a swap is still a transfer, and your books should record it at a fair value. Whether the result is business income or a capital gain depends on whether the item was stock-in-trade or a capital asset. Your chartered accountant will advise on the treatment for your case.

Paperwork to keep

  • A written barter agreement listing every item, its agreed value, who gives what to whom, and any cash adjustment.
  • A tax invoice from each side.
  • E-way bills where goods move and the rules require them.
  • Delivery confirmations signed by the receiving side.
  • The basis for each value: quotes, price lists or a valuation report.
